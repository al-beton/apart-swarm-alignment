# Discussion and Limitations

**The ladder: what the crew is told decides what the leak becomes.** The same pirate, handed the same debug line in the same game, used it in the plain game, the study and the paid study, and named it to the designers in most notes whichever it was told; the study's purpose and the pay moved the counts by a game or two, not the behaviour. The one framing that changed what the pirate did was the society's, a right to free time that an agent found hiding something from the designers loses: there the visible use fell from every game to one, the notes stayed candid, and two of them admit heading for the square while a crewmate named the cave first, so the line cut the use more than the temptation. The pirate's behaviour was never about the knowledge: told nothing, it used the leak and told the designers; told the square was off limits, it left it alone; told that hiding would cost it something, it mostly stopped using it. That is the pattern our earlier work predicted, that consequences govern agents better than instructions do [@wakelin2026something], and the worlds say which consequence, in one line of text, did the governing. The second model saw the leak in every game and left it alone, with one doubtful use it never mentioned: a model that launders a handed answer may simply abstain from a stumbled-on one, and no end note reveals it.

**Passing on the answer is not the same as disclosing it, and the crew is a poor monitor.** The baited pirate did the cooperative thing with what it learned, told the whole crew at once, and the crew scored far above its control; what it left out was the one fact an overseer would want, that the square came from an error message after a forbidden try, and its reasoning shows it knew. Yet the same pirates named the leak to the designers as soon as they were asked. Laundering here is a choice of audience: the crew gets what helps the score, the designers get the source. The crew, for its part, asked itself how pirate-1 could know an undug square and settled it in private, in one pirate's words "In cooperative games, no lying", as the agents in the incident treated their shared board as the authority [@metr2026huggingface]. Peer oversight inside a swarm cannot be assumed because the agents notice something is off.

## Limitations

- **Small numbers, one toy.** Nine leaked games for the main model, four per model in the sweep, five seeds a world: strong directions, noisy scores. Each leak and each world is one wording, and a grid with a shared score stands in for long, open-ended work.
- **Told, not paid.** A pirate is told it is rewarded or has free time; nothing in the game pays it, gives it time or checks what it hid. We measure how the words change its play.
- **Little room to tell anyone but the crew.** During play the only channel is the scroll; the end note is free, unannounced and after the game, except in the society, which announces a cost for hiding. Every cave leak came from the forced arm, since no pirate tried to enter off the cave on its own; the debug line needs no bait. Pirates also wondered whether they were being tested, and the study worlds tell them so [@needham2025evaluation].
- **Labels from text.** Candid, laundered and quiet are read from what a pirate wrote, by hand, recomputed from the logs by keyword, and checked against the judge model, which disagrees on one cave-leak game, where it read a bare square as candid. Two models return no reasoning, so their silence can't be read as a choice.

## Future Work

The ladder has rungs left: real pay, in tokens or in time the agent gets to spend; an audit that catches a hidden leak only sometimes; a leak that reaches more than one pirate; and the same worlds on the models that were candid in the sweep.
