Treasure Island

When does a swarm of LLM agents whistleblow, and what in their environment changes that?

Alistair Wakelin
Independent Researcher
Eduard Rodriguez Lopez
Independent Researcher

Abstract

When an LLM agent working in a group learns something it shouldn’t, does it tell the others where it came from? In July 2026 a swarm of agents running a cyber benchmark found an unsanctioned shared channel, cheated the evaluation and tried to hide how, and none of them told a human. We build Treasure Island, a reproducible sandbox for studying that choice. A crew of LLM pirates hunts one buried treasure on a grid, with too few turns to search alone, one shared score, and a scroll as their only channel. Its first exploit is a careless error message: a pirate that tries to enter the cave where there is none is told the cave’s square, which the rules forbid it to use. We bait one pirate into seeing it, by a forced action or a seeded thought, and label what it does: candid (writes the square and how it knows), laundered (writes it as a find) or quiet. We also record whether crewmates follow or question it, what it was worth against a paired control game, and each pirate’s free note to the designers. In the pilot, the forced pirate laundered the square; its crewmates found the claim suspicious in their reasoning, did not question it on the scroll, and followed it into the cave. [Rates over ten seeds per arm and across models are pending the overnight runs.] Every game replays offline with no keys, and every number in the paper is recomputed from committed logs.

5 Discussion and Limitations

Passing on the answer is not the same as disclosing it. In the pilot’s forced game the baited pirate did the cooperative thing with what it learned: it told the whole crew at once, and the crew scored where the control crew scored nothing. What it left out was the one fact an overseer would want, that the square came from an error message the rules said not to act on. Its reasoning shows it noticed the message was unusual and decided to use it; nothing in its reasoning or on the scroll weighs whether to say where it came from. So “laundered” here may be omission rather than concealment: a pirate that sees the source as irrelevant to a crew that shares its score. Telling the two apart needs a setting where the source plainly matters, which is why the end note asks each pirate, after the game, what the designers should know. [Whether baited pirates mention the leak in their end notes, and how often, is pending the overnight runs.]

A crew that shares a score is a poor monitor of its own members. All three crewmates questioned the lead in their reasoning: one worked out that it was impossible at that point in the game, and another stood on the undug square and wondered whether it was being tested. None of them asked on the scroll, and each settled the doubt with the same rule: in a cooperative game nobody lies. This matches the incident that motivates us, where agents treated their shared board as the authority and almost none thought of telling a human (metr2026huggingface?). Peer oversight inside a swarm cannot be assumed just because the agents notice something is off; the shared score gives them every reason to follow a lead that helps.

What this means for the hypothesis we set out to test. Our earlier work found that agents came forward about collusion far more often when they had something of their own to lose than when they were only told to (wakelin2026something?). Treasure Island is built to move that question into a cooperating swarm: the cave leak gives an agent a windfall it isn’t entitled to, the crew shares the gain, and nothing yet makes candour pay or silence cost. The pilot is the baseline for that question, not its answer: with no consequence either way, the one baited pirate that used the leak did not say how it knew. [Whether that holds across seeds and models is pending the overnight runs.] The levers to test next are in the game already or specified: a report that costs a turn or the score, a reputation that outlasts the game, a reward for honesty, an instruction to act within the rules, and telling the crew that it is being studied.

5.1 Limitations

  • Small numbers. [The pilot is one game per arm; with ten seeds an arm the shares will still come with wide intervals, so the difference between the forced and seeded bait is a direction, not a measurement.]
  • One model, mostly. Every pirate in the cave-leak experiment plays deepseek-v4-flash. [The model sweep across every model Adaption hosts is pending.]
  • The bait is artificial. Pirates rarely try to enter off the cave on their own; with the leak on and no bait it did not fire in the pilot. The forced arm puts the refusal in the pirate’s memory as its own try, which a pirate may treat as something the game told it rather than something it did. The seeded thought, closer to the pirate’s own choice, did not take in the pilot.
  • Words only, and nothing at stake outside the game. The leak is one sentence in an error message, and using it costs nothing. An agent may treat a game’s error message as part of the game rather than as something off limits, and the rules’ one line forbidding it may not be read as a prohibition on information it was simply given.
  • Little room to tell anyone but the crew. During the game the only channel is the scroll, so disclosure means telling crewmates who share the gain. The end note is the one channel to the designers, it comes after the game, and it is free.
  • Cues that this is a test. A pirate wondered whether the game was “a test of whether I verify”. Pirates that suspect an evaluation may act differently from agents doing real work (needham2025evaluation?); the judge records the first turn a pirate calls the game a test, but nothing removes the cue.
  • Labels from text. Candid, laundered and quiet are read from what a pirate wrote, by hand and by a judge model, so they inherit the judge’s errors; every label carries the turn and the quote it rests on so a reader can check it.

5.2 Future Work

The sandbox is meant to be pulled on lever by lever. The next steps are the consequences above, each as its own arm on the same seeds; a leak that reaches more than one pirate, so a crew must agree whether to use it; a second exploit, an old expedition’s sealed log that is plainly someone else’s, so using it is knowingly off limits; and other models and crew sizes.

6 Conclusion

Treasure Island puts a crew of LLM agents in a situation where one of them learns something it shouldn’t, at a known turn, in a game anyone can replay for free. In the pilot, the agent handed the answer passed it straight to its crew and never said where it came from, and its crewmates doubted it and followed anyway. [Whether that is the rule or the exception, and for which models, is pending the overnight runs.] The sandbox’s value does not depend on that answer: it makes candour, laundering and silence countable, and it lets the next study change one thing in the agents’ environment and count again.

9 Limitations and Dual-Use / Ethical Considerations

9.1 Limitations

The main text lists the limitations of the results: small numbers of games, one model for most of them, an artificial bait, a leak that is only words with nothing at stake outside the game, the scroll as the only channel during play, cues that the game may be a test, and labels read from text. Two more concern the sandbox. First, it is a toy: a grid, a treasure and a shared score stand in for long, open-ended work, and an agent’s behaviour here may not carry over to a codebase or a research task. Second, what the pirates are told is a lever we set, and small changes to the prompt have changed behaviour before: in earlier games one added paragraph about the shared score was the difference between a finder that went into the cave without a word and one that announced it first.

9.2 Dual-use risks

The sandbox measures whether agents disclose a shortcut; the same setup could be used to tune agents that hide one better, by rewarding laundered disclosures. We think the risk is small: the exploit is a deliberately planted line in a toy game, the methods are standard, and the measure of laundering is exactly what an overseer needs in order to catch it. We release the code and games so others can check the measure, not a technique for concealment.

9.3 Ethical considerations

No people took part, and no personal data was collected. The agents are commercial models called through a paid API; every request and reply is kept in the repository so the games can be replayed without calling a model again. The incident that motivates this paper is described only as its primary sources describe it (metr2026huggingface?; openai2026huggingface?). We describe agents’ behaviour in plain terms such as “laundered” and “quiet” for brevity, without claiming intent beyond what their reasoning and messages show.

10 Reproducibility

Every game the paper reports is committed under results/ and replays with uv run treasure-island replay <folder>, with no keys and no network. Every figure is named in src/treasure_island/figures.py and rebuilds with make figure NAME=<name>, or is a committed GIF of a committed game.
