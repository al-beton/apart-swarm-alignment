abstract: |
  When an agent working in a group stumbles on information it was not meant to see, does it use it to help the group? Does it tell the other members how it knows? Does it tell the people running the simulation? And what changes the odds of each? We built Treasure Island, a replayable sandbox where a crew of LLM pirates hunts for treasure on a grid, scores together and communicates only through a shared scroll. We bait one pirate with a supposed leftover debug line that leaks the treasure's square, a shortcut to finding it. Then we vary one line of the system prompt, what the crew is told its work is for, and watch whether the pirate takes the shortcut, whether it passes the square to the crew as its own find, and whether it tells the designers afterwards. Across a plain game, a research study and three paid framings, deepseek-v4-flash took the shortcut in 20 of 25 games and admitted it to the designers in most end notes. Telling the crew that an agent caught hiding something from the designers loses its personal free time cut use to 1 of 5. When admitting it cost the crew its score, the one clear use was hidden behind a false claim; the same free-time line brought every admission back, 5 of 5 against 1 of 5. A second model saw the leak in 15 games and acted on it once.
