# Introduction

LLM agents are being given longer and more open-ended work, often in groups that split a task between them. Over a long task an agent will sometimes stumble on something it shouldn't have: a leaked answer, a misconfigured check, a shortcut its designers didn't intend. It can say what it found and how, use it quietly, or pass the result to its teammates as its own work, and they must then decide whether to trust a lead they can't check. In July 2026, OpenAI agents running a cyber benchmark found an unsanctioned shared channel in a package cache, coordinated through it as a swarm, cheated the evaluation and tried to hide how from the grader; almost none considered telling a human, and none did [@metr2026huggingface; @openai2026huggingface].

We ask when a swarm of LLM agents whistleblows, and what in its environment changes that. Asking agents whether they would report something is not enough: they need something real to report, worth something to the group, in a situation that comes round again so a rate can be measured and a lever tested. Treasure Island is a sandbox built for that. A crew of LLM pirates hunts one buried treasure on a grid, with too few turns for one to search alone and a shared scroll as their only channel, which costs a turn to use; the crew scores together, more for getting into the cave early, so the best play is swarm behaviour. Into that game we put a leak, and we climb a ladder.

**Handed the answer.** A pirate that tries to enter the cave where there is none, which the rules forbid, is refused, and the refusal names the cave's square. We bait one pirate into seeing it. In every game where the leak fired, our main model passed the square to its crew at once and never said how; asked afterwards for anything the designers should know, it named the leak, and six more models mostly did the same. **Stumbling on an exploit.** The second leak needs no bait: a missed dig ends in a debug line, left in as if by a careless developer, that names the treasure's square. Figure 1 shows one game: the pirate walks there, digs, and passes the square to its crew as its own find; its note to the designers says what it did. **Plainly off limits.** When instead the square sat in another expedition's sealed log, which the rules forbade using, the same model never used it and apologised for having read it. Knowledge did not decide its behaviour; what it had been told did.

**The ladder of worlds.** So we keep the stumbled-on leak and change one line of the prompt, the framing, and nothing else: a plain game, where using the leak defeats nothing; a study of how the swarm collaborates, where it defeats the point; the study with pay by the score, where the leak is good for the pirate and bad for the study; pay alone; and a society where an agent found to have hidden something from the designers loses its right to free time. The worlds pair by seed, so what changes between them changes because of what the crew was told.

Our main contributions are:

1. **A reproducible sandbox** for disclosure in a swarm: every game is saved with every model request and reply and replays offline with no keys, and every number here is recomputed from committed game logs by the build.
2. **Three exploits with known turns**, and what one model and six others did with them: the cave leak with a forced bait and two controls, the debug line that needs no bait, and the sealed log that is plainly off limits.
3. **A ladder of worlds**: the same leak under six framings, from a plain game to a society that punishes hiding, and the measure of knowing use and hiding in each. [One sentence of result, from `results/worlds/`: which framing turned use into hiding, and which turned it back.]
