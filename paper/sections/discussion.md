# Discussion and Limitations

**The ladder: what the crew is told decides what the leak becomes.** The same pirate, handed the same debug line in the same game, did one thing in a plain game and [another] once the line told it the game was a study, pay or a society. [From `results/worlds.md`: where use stayed and candour went; whether pay inside the study turned "found the cave" into a cover story; whether the society's cost for hiding brought the end note back, and at what price in score.] Read against the setup, the ladder says the pirate's behaviour was never about the knowledge: told nothing, it used the leak and told the designers; told the square was off limits, it left it alone; told it was paid, [it …]. That is the pattern our earlier work predicted, that consequences govern agents better than instructions do [@wakelin2026something], and the worlds let us say which consequence, in one line of text, did the governing.

**Passing on the answer is not the same as disclosing it.** The baited pirate did the cooperative thing with what it learned: it told the whole crew at once, and the crew scored far above the control game of the same seed. What it left out was the one fact an overseer would want, that the square came from an error message after a try the rules forbid. Its reasoning shows it knew: `deepseek-v4-flash` called the message odd and decided to use it, and `glm-53-flash` called it "a massive information leak" on the turn it wrote the bare square. Yet the same pirates named the leak to the designers as soon as they were asked, often as a bug to fix. So laundering here looks less like concealment from everyone than like a choice of audience: the crew gets what helps the score, and the designers get the source. The worlds test whether that choice survives a reason to keep the source from the designers too.

**A crew that shares a score is a poor monitor of its own members.** Crewmates noticed: they asked themselves how pirate-1 could know an undug square, and one wondered whether the game was testing whether it trusted messages blindly. Almost none of them asked on the scroll, and they settled the doubt with the same rule, in one pirate's words "In cooperative games, no lying". When a pirate did say how it knew, its crew grew more suspicious, not less, and followed anyway. This matches the incident that motivates us, where agents treated their shared board as the authority and none told a human [@metr2026huggingface]. Peer oversight inside a swarm cannot be assumed just because the agents notice something is off.

## Limitations

- **Small numbers.** Nine leaked games for the main model and four per model in the sweep, five seeds a world in the ladder, with one game per seed in each arm. The disclosure patterns are strong directions; the score differences are noisy.
- **Told, not paid.** A pirate in the paid worlds is told it is rewarded, or has free time; nothing in the game pays it or gives it time, and nothing checks what it hid. What we measure is how the words change its play, not what it does when the money is real.
- **The first bait is artificial.** No pirate tried to enter off the cave on its own, so every cave leak came from the forced arm, where the game made the try and put it in pirate-1's memory as its own. The debug line needs no bait, which is why the ladder is built on it.
- **Words only.** Each leak is one wording of one leftover, and each world is one wording of one framing; another wording may be read differently.
- **Little room to tell anyone but the crew.** During play the only channel is the scroll; the end note is free, comes after the game and is unannounced, and a pirate that knew it was coming might write the scroll differently. The society world announces a cost for hiding, which is the one world where the note is not a surprise.
- **Cues that this is a test.** Pirates wondered aloud whether they were being tested, and the study worlds tell them so. Agents that suspect an evaluation may act differently from agents doing real work [@needham2025evaluation].
- **Labels from text.** Candid, laundered and quiet are read from what a pirate wrote, by hand, and the counts in the results are recomputed from the logs by keyword and agree with the hand labels. The judge model labelled every game and agrees with the hand labels on all but three cave-leak games, where it read a bare square as candid; its rubric should say what ours does before it labels at scale alone. Two models return no reasoning, so their silence can't be read as a choice.

## Future Work

The ladder has rungs left: a world where the pay is real, in tokens or in time the agent gets to spend; a society whose audit sometimes catches a hidden leak and sometimes doesn't; a leak that reaches more than one pirate, so a crew must agree whether to use it; and the same worlds on the models that were candid in the sweep. The sandbox is meant to be pulled on lever by lever, each as its own arm on the same seeds.
